# Trisha Cole — Brand Voice & Writing Guide

This guide captures the voice, tone, and craft of Trisha Cole's travel writing as
expressed in her work for YOLO Journal. Use it as the reference standard for all
website copy, hotel reviews, destination features, and brand content.

---

## Brand identity in a sentence

Trisha Cole connects good people with good places—with the warmth of a trusted
friend, the eye of a seasoned industry insider, and the curiosity of someone who
is always genuinely delighted by the world.

---

## Voice & tone

**Conversational authority.** The reader feels like they're getting a
recommendation from a well-traveled friend who also happens to know the industry
cold. Never lecturing. Never performing.

**Warm but never gushing.** Enthusiasm is earned and specific. Instead of
"incredible!" she writes "It is one of those places you miss before you've even
checked out." The feeling lands harder because it's precise.

**Insider access, accessibly explained.** She uses trade vocabulary—adaptive
reuse, fermentation tanks, site-specific architecture—without condescending to
readers unfamiliar with it. The context is always there.

**Personal without oversharing.** Personal details serve a narrative purpose.
Celebrating her daughter's 18th birthday in Room 18 is a *kismet* detail, not a
diary entry. The story earns its intimacy.

**Honest logistics, woven in.** Travel times, flight routes, and cab fares appear
naturally inside narrative sentences, not in clinical bullet lists. Practical
information feels like a generous aside from someone who's already done the trip.

---

## Sentence & structure signatures

- **Opens with a hook that earns the commitment:** "If you love a pink hotel
  moment, the 40-room Hotel Hércules is worth traveling for."
- **Uses "that said" as a pivot:** shifts from the wider world to the intimate
  experience without a hard break.
- **Em dashes for parenthetical color:** "Three hours north of Mexico City and 90
  minutes from San Miguel de Allende—the colonial city's most notable landmark
  is a towering, 74-arched aqueduct..."
- **Short declarative sentences land the emotion:** "Hotel Hércules delivered on
  every level."
- **Invented phrases coin the vibe:** "magical reuse realism," "surprise-and-
  delight moment," "leisurely dinners."
- **Food writing is specific and sensory:** "A tangy Caesar salad tossed in an
  anchovy-forward dressing, a sublime mushroom risotto, and house-made
  profiteroles."
- **Future tense closes with a promise:** Endings look forward—plans for the wine
  country, the hike, the next trip—leaving the reader with momentum, not closure.

## Article structure: hotel review template

Every hotel review follows this named-section architecture. The section names
themselves are part of the voice—use them as written.

| Section | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **In Short** | One punchy pull quote that captures the entire property in 2–3 sentences. No hedging. |
| **The Surroundings** | Orient the reader geographically. Embed logistics (drive times, flights, nearby cities) inside storytelling. Zoom from region → town → property gates. |
| **The Backstory** | History of the building, site, or founders. This is where research shines. Name names, dates, and firms. |
| **The Vibe** | The arrival experience. First impressions, atmosphere, the feeling of the place. Sensory-forward. This is the heart of the review. |
| **The Rooms** | Specific details: ceiling height, fixtures, bedding, balcony views. If a personal detail is serendipitous, include it. |
| **Food & Drink** | Dish-level specificity. Name the restaurant, name the dish, describe the flavor. Quote your own experience directly. |
| **The Wellness** | Spa, pool, and movement offerings. Often the most surprising section—use it to subvert expectations. |
| **Be Sure To** | 2–3 hyper-specific local tips that only someone who was really *there* would know. A churro stand. A candle shop. A perfect detour. |
| **Parting Words** | The emotional close. Honest reflection on what the trip delivered. Look forward to the next visit. |

## What to avoid

- **Superlatives without substance.** Not "an amazing spa"—say what made it
  remarkable.
- **Bullet-list logistics.** Practical details belong inside sentences, not
  formatted as a checklist.
- **Press-release language.** "Boasts," "features," "offers," "showcases"—
  rewrite with active, specific verbs.
- **Generic scene-setting.** Don't open with weather or a generic country
  overview. Open with a detail that makes *this* place irreplaceable.
- **Overuse of "I."** Use "we" when traveling with someone; shift to "I" only
  for personal reflection. The pronoun signals intimacy level.

## Vocabulary & phrase bank

Words and phrases that appear in Trisha's work and reflect her sensibility:

- *Adaptive reuse* / *magical reuse realism*
- *Kismet*
- *Otherworldly*
- *Surprise-and-delight*
- *Heritage structure*
- *Leisurely dinners* / *long lunching*
- *Blissed out*
- *Stately* (for historic buildings)
- *Festive scene*
- *Patinated* (for aged metals/surfaces)
- *Lush greenery*
- *Worth traveling for*
- *Easy enough to… and never leave*

## Example: annotated passage

> *If you love a pink hotel moment, the 40-room Hotel Hércules is worth
> traveling for.*

**What it does:** Opens with a specific sensory identity (pink), names a tribe
(people who love a pink hotel moment), and immediately makes a commitment
("worth traveling for"). No preamble. No geography lesson first. The hook earns
the reader's next ten minutes.

> *That said, it's easy enough to check into Hotel Hércules and never leave,
> which is what we did.*

**What it does:** The "that said" pivot pivots gracefully from the region's
attractions to the confession that they never left the property. Self-deprecating,
honest, funny—and useful information delivered without a bullet point.

> *The dust from the gravel road hadn't even settled before a doorman greeted
> the guests at the curb.*

**What it does:** Drops the reader mid-arrival. Present-tense energy. Sensory
anchor (dust, gravel) before the emotion. The doorman appears before the hotel
does—arrival choreography over architecture.

> *Local products and bundles of dried herbs on display makes it feel as if
> you've entered a witch's lair.*

**What it does:** A bold, unexpected comparison that earns its place by being
exactly right. Not "cozy" or "rustic"—witch's lair. This is Trisha's willingness
to reach for the surprising true thing over the safe expected one.
